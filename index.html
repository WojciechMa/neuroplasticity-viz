Neuroplasticity course: Activity-dependent synaptic refinement

The visualisation below was created as part of a Neuroplasticity course. It shows how the relative timing of pre- and postsynaptic firing decides which developing synapses are kept and which are pruned.

During development, axons initially form redundant, diffuse connections. Spontaneous retinal waves then refine them: synapses whose pre- and postsynaptic neurons fire in phase are potentiated and stabilised, while those whose partners fire out of phase are depressed and pruned.

Choose a scenario, press Start retinal wave, and follow the synaptic weights. All parameters can be changed while the simulation runs.

Each population's firing rate oscillates between silent and maximal, fX times per second; the phase offset sets when the peaks occur. The result is clipped to stay between 0 and 1. A silent retina is simply rR=0.

Real retinal waves are bursts of correlated firing separated by long silent intervals; the sinusoid is a simplification, and simulated time is compressed.

Each neuron lags slightly behind its neighbour, so the peak of activity propagates across the population, as a retinal wave sweeps across the retina. This only affects the display: the learning rule uses the centre neuron, and at u=12 this is exactly equation 1.

3. Noise: Ornstein–Uhlenbeck process
ξ←ξ−ξτΔt +2Δtτ⋅z
SymbolMeaningSet by

This is the Euler–Maruyama update of an Ornstein–Uhlenbeck process: the middle term relaxes ξ towards 0 and the last term adds Gaussian increments, giving temporally correlated noise rather than independent jumps. The Noise slider σ scales it in equation 1.

The noise is independent between populations, so it adds no correlation between them: it blurs the timing but leaves the mean drive unchanged, apart from small effects where a rate is clipped at 0 or 1.

The weight saturates at 1 (shown as “stabilised”). If it falls below 0.05, the synapse is pruned and stays eliminated until Restart.

Plasticity is gated by presynaptic activity. When the retina fires and the target fires with it, the synapse is potentiated (coincident firing triggers BDNF release); when the retina fires but the target does not, it is depressed. While the retina is silent the weights do not change, consistent with the finding that blocking activity before birth leaves retinal projections unsegregated rather than eliminating them (Shatz & Stryker, 1988).

This combines Hebb's postulate (“cells that fire together, wire together”) with its complement, that inputs firing out of sync with their target are weakened (Stent, 1973), often summarised as “out of sync, lose your link”.

6. Derivation: mean drive = ½ cos Δφ

Take equal frequencies and no noise, write ω=2πf, and set t = 0 at a rising zero crossing of the retinal oscillation:

rR=12+12sinωt rX=12+12sin(ωt+Δφ)

Step 1. Substitute into the drive from equation 5 and expand:

pX= (1+sinωt)sin(ωt+Δφ) = sin(ωt+Δφ) + sinωt⋅sin(ωt+Δφ)

Step 2. Apply the product-to-sum identity sina⋅sinb=12[cos(a−b)−cos(a+b)]:

sinωt⋅sin(ωt+Δφ) = 12cosΔφ − 12cos(2ωt+Δφ)

Step 3. Take the time average over a full cycle, written ⟨ ⟩. Every term that still depends on t oscillates symmetrically and averages to 0, leaving only the constant term:

⟨pX⟩= 12cosΔφ
ΔφMean driveEffect on w
0° (in phase)+0.50fastest potentiation
60°+0.25slow potentiation
90° (quadrature)0no net change
120°−0.25slow depression
180° (anti-phase)−0.50fastest depression

Frequency mismatch. With ωR≠ωX and an initial phase difference Δφ0, step 2 becomes

sinωRt⋅sin(ωXt+Δφ0) = 12cos((ωX−ωR)t+Δφ0) − 12cos((ωX+ωR)t+Δφ0)

Both terms now depend on t, so the long-run mean drive is 0. The first term varies slowly, at the beat frequency (see 4), so the weight still oscillates for several seconds at a time without net change.

In the readout table, “Expected mean drive” is this formula and “Mean Hebbian drive, last 6 s” is the value measured in the simulation; they agree up to noise.

7. Competition between the two synapses

Suppose targets A and B fire in anti-phase. A 180° shift inverts a sinusoid, so with equal frequencies and no noise:

φB=φA+180° ⇒ rB=1−rA ⇒ pB=−pA ⟨pA⟩=12cosΔφ ⟨pB⟩=−12cosΔφ

Here Δφ is the phase difference between the retina and A. At every moment the drive on R→B is the negative of the drive on R→A, so the retina can be in phase with only one target: one synapse is stabilised and the other is pruned.

  • Retina in quadrature (90°) with both targets: both mean drives are 0 and neither synapse is selected.
  • Targets in phase with each other: both synapses receive the same drive and both are stabilised, so there is no selection.

Anti-correlated activity in the targets is therefore what turns Hebbian plasticity into competition between synapses.

8. Time to stabilisation or pruning

Averaging over the fluctuations within each cycle, the weight changes linearly in time:

wX(t)≈w0+ η⋅12cosΔφX⋅t

Solving for the time at which it reaches 1 (stabilised) or 0.05 (pruned):

Tstable= 1−w0η⋅12cosΔφX Tprune= w0−0.05η⋅12|cosΔφX|

With the default settings (w0=0.5, η=0.1) in the anti-phase scenario, A is in phase with the retina, so Tstable=0.5/0.05=10 s. B is in anti-phase with the retina, so Tprune=0.45/0.05=9 s.

In the weight plot the trace changes in steps rather than smoothly, because the drive acts only while the retina fires. Doubling the learning rate halves both times; the closer |Δφ| is to 90°, the longer they become, and at exactly 90° neither occurs.

9. Numerical method and display

The simulation moves forward in steps of Δt=1/240 of a simulated second. At every step it updates each population and both synapses (forward Euler method):

θX←θX+2πfXΔt wX←min(1,wX+ηpXΔt)
  • Adding 2πfΔt at every step, instead of computing 2πft, means that changing a frequency does not introduce a phase discontinuity.
  • The Simulation speed slider sets how many simulated seconds pass per real second.
  • “Mean Hebbian drive, last 6 s” is the mean of pX over the last 6 s of retinal activity. Six seconds is three full cycles at 0.5 Hz, so the within-cycle fluctuations cancel.

Display only, not part of the model:

  • Line width grows with wX, and neuron brightness shows the firing rate.
  • A synapse is drawn teal while its drive is above +0.3 (potentiation) and coral while it is below −0.3 (depression); the rate plots are shaded the same way.
  • Green BDNF particles appear while the drive is above 0.5 (on average 7×pX per second) and travel retrogradely, from the target to the retina.
